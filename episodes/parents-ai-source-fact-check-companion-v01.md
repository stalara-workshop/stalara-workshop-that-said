## Production integrity note

This episode is intentionally practical and parent-facing. The script should avoid sounding like a research report, but any specific statistics, platform features, age rules, or safety claims should be re-verified shortly before recording and again before publication if the release is delayed.

AI platform features and youth-safety policies are changing quickly. Do not assume parental controls, teen settings, quiet hours, memory settings, account linking, age gates, or safety alert behavior remain unchanged.

# 1. Teen AI chatbot use and parent awareness gap

## Claim supported

Teen AI chatbot use is already common, and many parents underestimate or are unsure about whether their teen uses chatbots.

## Suggested script usage

> “Teen AI use is not hypothetical anymore. Current survey data shows that a majority of U.S. teens have used AI chatbots, and many parents either underestimate that use or are not sure whether it is happening.”

## Primary source to verify

Pew Research Center, February 2026: teen and parent survey on AI chatbot use.

## Facts to verify before recording

- Exact percentage of U.S. teens ages 13–17 who report using AI chatbots.
- Exact percentage of parents who say their teen uses AI chatbots.
- Exact percentage of parents who are unsure whether their teen uses AI chatbots.
- Survey field dates and sample size.

## Current working figures from development

- 64% of U.S. teens report using AI chatbots.
- 51% of parents say their teen uses chatbots.
- Roughly three-in-ten parents are unsure whether their teen uses chatbots.
- Pew teen/parent survey fielded September 25–October 9, 2025, published February 2026.

## Caution

Use “chatbots” or “AI chatbots” rather than “AI” when citing these specific statistics. Do not generalize chatbot statistics to all AI tools or all children.

# 2. How teens use AI chatbots

## Claim supported

Teens use AI chatbots for more than cheating. Major reported uses include information search, schoolwork help, entertainment, summarizing, media creation, casual conversation, and emotional support/advice.

## Suggested script usage

> “When adults hear ‘AI and school,’ a lot of them immediately hear ‘cheating.’ Cheating is part of the story. But if that is the only story we tell, we miss what kids are actually doing.”

## Primary source to verify

Pew Research Center, February 2026: “How teens use and view AI” / teen AI chatbot use survey.

## Facts to verify before recording

- Percentage using chatbots to search for information.
- Percentage using chatbots for schoolwork help.
- Percentage using chatbots for fun or entertainment.
- Percentage using chatbots to summarize articles/books/videos.
- Percentage using chatbots to create or edit images/videos.
- Percentage using chatbots for casual conversation.
- Percentage using chatbots for emotional support/advice.

## Current working figures from development

- 57% use chatbots for searching information.
- 54% use chatbots for help with schoolwork.
- 47% use chatbots for fun or entertainment.
- About 40% use chatbots for summarizing articles/books/videos.
- About 40% use chatbots for creating or editing images/videos.
- 16% use chatbots for casual conversation.
- 12% use chatbots for emotional support or advice.

## Caution

Do not frame emotional-support use as the dominant use. It is smaller than information, schoolwork, and entertainment, but significant enough to discuss seriously.

# 3. Schoolwork and cheating

## Claim supported

Schoolwork is a common teen AI use, and cheating concerns are legitimate, but “AI use = cheating” is too blunt.

## Suggested script usage

> “The question is not just ‘Did AI touch this assignment?’ The question is ‘Did my kid do the learning?’”

## Primary source to verify

Pew Research Center, February 2026 teen AI survey.

## Facts to verify before recording

- Percentage of teens who report using AI chatbots for schoolwork help.
- Percentage who say students at their school use AI chatbots to cheat at least somewhat often.
- Whether Pew directly asked teens if they personally cheated, or asked about perceptions of peers.

## Current working figures from development

- 54% of teens have used chatbots for schoolwork help.
- 59% of teens say students at their school use AI chatbots to cheat at least somewhat often.
- Pew’s cheating figure concerns teen perceptions of peer behavior, not necessarily self-reported cheating.

## Caution

Do not claim “59% of teens cheat with AI.” That is not what the survey says.

# 4. AI companions as a distinct risk category

## Claim supported

AI companions should be treated differently from general-purpose homework/search chatbots because they are designed for ongoing friendship, roleplay, romance, emotional intimacy, or relationship-like interaction.

## Suggested script usage

> “A homework chatbot, a search-integrated AI assistant, and an AI companion designed for emotional intimacy are not the same parenting problem.”

## Primary source to verify

Common Sense Media, July 2025: teen AI companion report, “Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs: How and Why Teens Use AI Companions.”

## Facts to verify before recording

- Teen sample size and ages.
- Percentage of teens who have used AI companions.
- Percentage who use them regularly.
- Percentage who have used them for serious conversations or chosen them over humans for serious conversations.
- Percentage who have shared personal information.
- Current Common Sense Media recommendation for under-18 AI companion use.

## Current working figures from development

- Survey of 1,060 U.S. teens ages 13–17, fielded April–May 2025.
- 72% of teens had used AI companions at least once.
- More than half used them regularly / at least a few times per month.
- About one-third had discussed serious topics with AI companions or used them for serious conversations.
- About one-quarter had shared personal information.
- Common Sense Media recommended that people under 18 not use AI companions in their current form.

## Caution

Do not say “AI companions are always harmful.” Better: “Current evidence and child-safety groups raise enough concern that parents should treat companion bots differently from homework tools.”

# 5. Family media guidance and parental mediation

## Claim supported

The episode’s parenting stance should be framed as active guidance plus boundaries, not total prohibition or total permissiveness.

## Suggested script usage

> “A rule your child can quietly route around is not the same thing as safety.”

> “The goal is guided access, clear rules, ongoing conversation, and more involvement as the stakes get higher.”

## Sources to verify

- American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren family media plan guidance.
- AAP guidance emphasizing age-aware, family-specific rules rather than one universal screen-time rule.
- Research on parental mediation of online/media use, especially active mediation and restrictive mediation.

## Facts / points to verify before recording

- Current AAP Family Media Plan language.
- Whether AAP has AI-specific guidance published since the script draft.
- Whether any wording has changed around screen-time limits and family plans.

## Caution

Do not say “bans never work.” Better: “Restrictions can have a place, especially by age and risk category, but silent prohibition is weak as a whole strategy.”

# 6. OpenAI / ChatGPT age guidance and parental controls

## Claim supported

ChatGPT has age guidance and parental-control features, but platform settings should not be framed as a substitute for parenting, school policy, or real-world support.

## Suggested script usage

> “Use the settings. Turn on the safeguards. Configure the tools together. But do not outsource the relationship.”

## Primary source to verify

OpenAI Help Center pages on:

- ChatGPT age requirements / whether ChatGPT is safe for all ages.
- ChatGPT parental controls.
- Teen account linking.
- Quiet hours.
- Memory / personalization controls.
- Safety alerts or parent notifications.
- Custom instructions and user settings.

## Facts to verify before recording

- Current minimum age for ChatGPT use.
- Whether users ages 13–18 require parental consent.
- Current parental-control capabilities.
- Whether parent-linked accounts allow quiet hours.
- Whether parents can view teen conversations. Do not imply they can unless official docs explicitly say so.
- Whether teens can unlink accounts and what notification occurs.
- Current behavior of safety alerts, if mentioned.

## Current working understanding from development

- ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13.
- Users ages 13–18 require parental consent.
- Parental controls can include parent/teen account linking, setting management, quiet hours, and some safety alert behavior.
- Platform controls have limits and can change.

## Caution

Do not present ChatGPT as “safe for teens” in a blanket way. Use platform safeguards as one tool among many.

# 7. Custom instructions / user settings as family agreement

## Claim supported

Where tools support custom instructions, user preferences, personalization, or family settings, parents and teens can co-create guidance that encourages learning, privacy, honesty, and real-world escalation.

## Suggested script usage

> “Settings are not a substitute for relationship. But when parents and kids write them together, they can become a family agreement the AI helps reinforce.”

## Sources to verify

- Platform documentation for custom instructions/user preferences.
- Platform documentation for teen/parent settings.
- Any current limitations on custom instructions for teen accounts.

## Caution

This is a recommendation from the episode, not a clinically validated intervention. Frame it as a practical family practice grounded in active guidance, transparency, and shared norms.

Do not imply custom instructions are guaranteed safety controls. They can help orient the AI, but they are not enforcement mechanisms.

# 8. Emotional support, social advice, and crisis topics

## Claim supported

Some teens use AI chatbots for casual conversation or emotional support. This can reflect real needs, but AI should not become the only place a child takes distress, conflict, self-harm thoughts, abuse, exploitation, threats, or feeling unsafe.

## Suggested script usage

> “AI can help with a question. It cannot become the whole support system.”

> “A child should not be alone with AI in a crisis.”

## Sources to verify

- Pew 2026 teen chatbot survey for general chatbot emotional support/advice use.
- Common Sense Media 2025 companion report for companion-specific serious conversation and personal information sharing.
- Current crisis-resource wording and local emergency guidance.

## Caution

Do not suggest AI is a therapist for children or teens. Do not give clinical advice. Use general guidance: bring in a trusted adult, clinician, school counselor, crisis line, emergency services, or other real-world support when safety is involved.

# 13. Tone guardrails

Avoid:

- “AI is coming for your children.”
- “Relax, it’s just a tool.”
- “Bans never work.”
- “AI companions are always harmful.”
- “ChatGPT is safe for teens.”
- “Parents should monitor everything.”
- “AI can be your kid’s therapist.”

Prefer:

- “This is already part of the world kids are growing up in.”
- “Restrictions can have a place, but prohibition alone is fragile.”
- “AI use is not one behavior.”
- “A general chatbot and an AI companion are different parenting problems.”
- “Use platform safeguards, but don’t outsource the relationship.”
- “Bring schoolwork back to teacher rules, facts back to sources, feelings back to trusted people, and safety back to real-world help.”
